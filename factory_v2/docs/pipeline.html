Every batch runs on parallel tracks.

/new-ba and /new-factory for Claude Code · design approved 26 Nov 2026 · new-ba built, new-factory in build

The old factory ran one issue at a time and split each issue into specs that waited on each other: a todo app took about five hours, and almost none of it was fixing bugs. The new pipeline moves the split to the start. The BA lays out a whole batch so most tickets build at once; the factory runs each on its own track, with one implementer, gates that are code rather than agents, and one QC at the end.

The BA lays out the tracks before any train leaves.

The old BA divided work by layer, “data model before the screens”, a chain where every ticket waited for the last. The new BA divides by behavior and pulls everything shared into one foundation ticket, so the tickets after it never touch each other.

Five words the BA thinks in

Slice
One thing a user can do from start to end: add a task, filter the list. Never a layer.
Owns
The only files a ticket may change. A folder is dir/**; its tests live inside it.
Foundation
Wave 0: types, interfaces, slots, registry, test setup, design tokens. No feature logic.
Contract
A shape the foundation provides and slices build on (TaskRepo, slot:input). Only added to after wave 0, never changed.
Wave
0 is the foundation, 1 needs only the foundation, 2 needs another slice’s behavior. At most three.

Twelve steps

  1. Understand the request; keep the user’s words in said.md; choose the design direction.
  2. Read the architecture, or choose one: a folder per feature and a registry that loads them.
  3. List the slices, at most about eight criteria each.
  4. Give each slice its Owns.
  5. Pull out the foundation: one ticket by default.
  6. Order the waves. Two slices that share a file merge; they are never queued.
  7. Write the tickets.
  8. Prototype the whole flow, with a design reviewer for at most two rounds.
  9. Check the batch with check-batch.mjs.
  10. One fresh reader for the whole batch.
  11. Publish the epic and its tickets.
  12. Revise after feedback, keeping every published criterion number.

Criteria are numbered across the batch: the foundation has C1–C4, the next ticket starts at C5.

How each criterion is written

  • Checkable from outside: someone holding only the ticket and the product says met or not met.
  • Every behavior that must hold, including the cases that go wrong.
  • One wording per rule, said once.
  • Behavior, not implementation, except a contract, which is exact.
  • Their words are not narrowed: “all”, “every”, “never” and numbers stay.
  • No open questions, no secrets, no real people’s data.

The foundation lays the rails for parallel runs

  • Dev server and e2e read PORT and BASE_URL; e2e always starts its own server.
  • E2e runs on the Playwright CLI with a JSON reporter.
  • CSS is scoped per feature.
  • Each feature declares its routes, storage keys and shortcuts; the registry fails a test on a duplicate.
  • Its tests assert only what later tickets will not change.

Size decides how much ceremony a ticket gets

SizeWhen
SCopy, config, a small bug: at most 3 criteria, no new screen, no auth, money or migration.
MA normal feature. The default.
LA complex new screen, or anything touching auth, money, migrations or security, however small.

The higher of size and risk. The factory checks it by script and only ever raises it.

One reader, then GitHub

A fresh agent, never a fork, asks three things: can each ticket be built without guessing, can two tickets that run together be built without knowing about each other, does the foundation hold every contract. Then publish-batch.mjs scans for secrets, creates the epic and its sub-issues, pushes the prototype and labels everything ready. The tickets are the source of truth; the plan in the epic is a summary built from them.

check-batch refuses

  • two tickets that can run at once and own the same file;
  • a dependency not on an earlier wave, or a cycle;
  • a criterion id in two tickets, or one rule said twice;
  • a contract used but not defined, or not from a ticket it depends on;
  • more than three waves without a reason;
  • a size below what the factory would give;
  • one library declared at two versions;
  • a foundation with e2e but no PORT criterion;
  • an Owns with no room for its tests;
  • a plan that does not fit plan-schema.json.

Follow one ticket down the line.

  1. Queue 0script

    One batch at a time on staging. A second waits and starts from the newest staging.

  2. Plan 1script

    Refuses without ready. Rebuilds the plan from the tickets with check-batch, seconds and no tokens. Edits since the BA are reported, not blocked.

  3. Prepare 2script

    Once per batch: adds every declared library, copies the prototype, installs once, clones node_modules copy-on-write into each worktree, starts two servers on two ports and checks for a browser.

  4. Schedule 3script

    A ticket starts as soon as what it depends on has merged. About four at once, each with its own worktree, port and cache.

  5. Build 4aagent

    One implementer, no planner, no spec. Codes, then one test per criterion named with its id (C5). Changes only what the ticket owns.

  6. Gates 4bscript

    The project’s checks; the diff against Owns, from the commits against this ticket’s own base; a test with a real assertion for every criterion.

  7. Review 4cagent

    S none, M one reviewer, L one on Opus plus a security review. The reviewer reads the tests too.

  8. Back to Build 4dscript + agent

    Two fix rounds, then a diagnosis, then a third round or needs-decision.

  9. Merge 5script

    Straight into staging with --no-ff. Lockfiles regenerated. Checked once when a dependent ticket is waiting on it.

  10. QC 6script + agent

    Once, when every ticket is done: a full check on the final staging, e2e reported per criterion, one agent using the app in a browser.

  11. Report 7script

    What was delivered, what waits on you and why, what changed since the BA.

  12. You 8you

    Check staging; send it to main when you are satisfied.

Code owns the skeleton. Agents own the judgment.

The old factory ran inside a Workflow script, which cannot read a file, run a command or tell the time, so every deterministic step became an agent: a checker that ran tests, a recorder that wrote the dashboard. The new orchestrator is a plain Node program with no dependencies. It keeps its state in state.json and calls claude -p only when judgment is needed.

The script decides

  • the order tickets start, from the dependency graph;
  • which files a ticket may change, and whether it did;
  • whether the checks passed, from their exit codes;
  • whether every criterion has a test;
  • every merge, revert, label and comment;
  • who a failure belongs to;
  • when an agent is stuck, and why;
  • the time, the cost and the dashboard.

Agents judge

  • Implementer: writes the code and its tests.
  • Reviewer: is the change right against its criteria, and do its tests test them.
  • Diagnoser: after two failed rounds, why it still fails.
  • Second reviewer: settles a dispute with the first.
  • QC: does the product do what the criteria say, used as a person would.

At most four fix rounds per ticket over the whole batch, counting those sent back after merge or by QC.

needs-decision, never “failed”

  • Its branch is kept.
  • Its issue gets the diagnosis and one concrete question, with the label needs-decision.
  • If it was merged, it is reverted from staging with its dependents, so staging holds only work that passed QC.
  • The rest of the batch carries on.
  • Once you answer, it resumes alone, reverting the revert if it had been merged, then its dependents, then QC on their criteria.

No timeouts. An alarm with a reason.

An agent with no progress for 30 minutes raises an alarm: ticket, stop, agent, how long, the cause, what was done, the last 20 log lines, as a macOS notification and a comment on the issue. Nothing is killed except a child process whose cause is recognised.
